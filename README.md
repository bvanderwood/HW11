## Part A - Problem outline and setup
This code addresses *Problem 2 - agent based modelling of pandemic spread*.  

### Part I - Environment and Initial Conditions
A 75x75 matrix is created via a function.  Each grid square can hold which agents are present there.  Parameters for the model are stored in a simulationConfig object to allow easy modification or running any number of simulations serially.  A variety of initial parameters were tried - with agent counts < 100 and a grid > 75x75, even if infectivity (p) were set to 1 (a gaurantee that sharing a cell with an infected agent would infect susceptible agents) and recovery (q) set to 0, it would take more than 200 steps to infect even 20% of the population.  

While all initial settings played a role in the height and timing of peak infectivity, the *starting number of infected, infectivity, and recovery* were the most important factors in non-social distancing simulations.  In real world clinical scenarios, we often think of infectivity in terms of R0 value  - how many individuals will an infected individual transmit to, on average (basic reproduction number).  This number is useful for two reasons: first, its easier to measure (knowing exactly how many individuals an infected person came in contact with can be challenging, isolating just the contacts who were infected and averaging them is a little easier); second, it gives us an idea of the exponentialness of spread.  R0's are also used to estimate herd immunity threshhold.  In our agentic model, we instead need a measure of transmission risk at some arbitrary time step.  You could abstract our time steps to a broader population model based on the frequency of agent-agent interactions to try and pin down the relationship between infectivity and R0.  Likewise, our recovery fraction gives agents a chance to stop being infectious at each time step and move into the recovered pool.  Clinically, we generally think of recovery from infectious illness as a normally distributed window with minimums and maximums.  Our more abstract "q" simplifies things like people who were somewhat immune getting exposed and recovering after brief, minimal illness (eg the outliers who recover after 1 time step) or the immunocompromised who could remain infected for weeks at a time into a single number.

### Part II - Agent Behaviors
In this model, our agents move semi-randomly within our grid along a Levy distribution.  In the initial model, they did not account for other agents and the Levy step size distribution was set (arbitrarily).  Each step, if they shared a cell with an infected agent they would have a probability of becoming infected, while those infected would have a probability of recovery.  In a more sophisticated model, we implemented avoidance behavior and social distancing.  Avoidance behavior makes agents aware of the agents in the cells around them and actively avoid those cells (it defines safe cells as those cells without an infected agent and then randomizes movement into a safe cell.  If there are no safe cells, the agent doesn't move).  Social distancing reduces the amount moved each step by altering the Levy distribution.  

### Part III - Simulation Results and Sensitivity Analysis
#### IIIa - Altered p and q
Altering p and q (and more specifically, the ratio between them) significantly altered the spread of our simulated pandemic.  Sufficiently high Q's, regardless of P, on a relatively sparse grid ensured R0 < 1 (ie, no spread, the initial infected quickly tapered to "recovered").  Lower Q's created room for the pandemic to grow, scaling with P and grid density.  Higher starting values of infected agents caused the peak to be reached sooner in the simulation run.  Higher P's also caused the peak to be reached sooner, and higher P/Q ratios increased the number of agents affected at peak.  

For a given P/Q ratio the mean number of affected were relatively similar across runs if other starting conditions were similar.  Likewise, the shape of the growth curve for infected was similar across runs, even when the initial number of infected were modified.  

Graphs of these runs with various starting conditions can be found in the Jupyter notebook in this repository.

#### IIIb - Social Distancing and Avoidance
Introduction of modified agent movement behavior had a dramatic impact on observed spread in our simulations.  Simple social distancing with modest reduction in movement lowered the peak number of cases and made it modestly later in the simulation.  More dramatic social distancing (movement reduction to < 10% of original versus ~33% of original) resulted in a similar peak number of cases, but a *much* later peak (step 330 vs step 140).  This could have important real-world implications in allowing health systems time to prepare and spreading the case load over a longer period of time.  Many of the deaths in the Covid-19 pandemic, particularly during the initial wave and the Delta variant wave, were a result of overwhelmed healthcare systems - people who perhaps could have been saved under different conditions instead died due to resource scarcity.

Avoidance behavior had an even more dramatic impact.  Even with relatively modest social distancing, active avoidance of infected individuals dramatically lowered the peak incidence.  In the real world this is challenging for many diseases - it may not be possible to completely avoid infected individuals, and perhaps more importantly it may be impossible to identify those in the early stages of an infection.  "Asymptomatic spreaders" are a feature of a number of infectious maladies, and other infection vectors like fomite spread may further blunt the avoidance effect.  You could introduce this to a model by rolling a probability for succesful avoidance that attempts to capture these factors.

I performed expanded social distance testing using a variety of movement limitations (a stand in for success/severity of social distancing measures; in real life these could translate to things like school closures, closure of event spaces, enforced 6 foot contact limits, etc).  I used more severe infectivity scores in these trials as social distancing is unlikely to be implemented in a low infectivity disease.  Across trials, we saw the same pattern repeated - small reductions in movement resulted in very modest reductions (or no reduction) in peak and time to peak infectivity.  Larger reductions in movement could substantially shift the time to peak.  While P and Q can also move the time to peak, they are intrinsic parts of the disease (at least for viral respiratory illnesses) and therefore not modifiable.  Thus severity of social distancing is the most significant population wide modifier that can be used to alter the maximum number of subjects in a given time window.  Depending on the initial number of cases, the peak _could_ wind up the same, just later in the run.  The total duration of the pandemic did also appear to be _longer_ with social distancing, although our total number of steps was often not long enough to catch this.  Significant social distancing also allowed us to sometimes see the effects of reaching herd immunity - there were so many patients in the R pool versus the S pool that transmission slowed to a halt.  For very severe social distancing (movement probability to 0.01) even the most severe starting conditions (40% of agents infected, P of 1, Q of 0.001) did not result in exponential spread.
